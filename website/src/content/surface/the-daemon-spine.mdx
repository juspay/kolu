A surface in a browser tab dies with the tab. A surface in a **daemon** outlives
every client that connects to it, and outlives its own code. You ship a new
build while a client is mid-session, and the old process still holds the socket.
The daemon stratum makes that moment safe.

## What a daemon must answer that a page does not

A page never has to say which version of itself you are talking to. There is one
version, and it reloads. A daemon has to say it constantly, because the process a
client is attached to and the build you just shipped can be different things. A
client that cannot tell them apart either drops every session on an upgrade, or
serves stale behaviour under the new build's name.

So a daemon needs two things a page does not. It needs a way to say which build
it is, which is what [How to bake an identity](/surface/bake-an-identity)
covers. And it needs a small part of itself that never changes shape, so the
question can be asked across a version mismatch. That part is the spine.

## Why the control core is frozen

Identity is read over a version-agnostic channel, before the versioned
handshake. That ordering is the whole trick. If reading a daemon's identity
required speaking its current contract, you could not read it in the one
situation where you need it most. Freezing the control core and probing it
before connecting lets a supervisor learn what it is looking at, then decide
whether to talk to it, recycle it, or drain it.

Everything behind the spine is free to churn. The surface can grow cells, the
handlers can change, the build can be replaced whole. The frozen core still
answers the same question the same way. The fields it answers with are listed in
[the daemon reference](/surface/ref-surface-daemon).

### What a frozen contract survives, and what it does not

The frozen contract holds within one protocol epoch, not across a change of
framing. Version negotiation happens inside the protocol being replaced, so a
peer from an earlier epoch cannot be asked anything at all. Its first frame does
not decode. A supervisor reports that as an unspeakable protocol, never as an
incompatible version, because a value carried inside a frame cannot describe a
break in the frame itself.

That is why the control core's version number stays put when the framing moves.
It versions the hello payload, and the payload's fields have not changed. A
framing change is a declared flag day: you stop every old process once, and from
there the frozen contract holds again.

## Recycling a daemon without lying to its clients

With a stable identity and a version-agnostic probe, a live upgrade is a
decision rather than a gamble. `converge` reads the running identity, compares it
to the build you baked, and acts by a declared policy. The client sees a brief
reconnect, not a dropped session.

The contract is the promise the daemon keeps across that swap. A recycle may
replace every byte of the process, but it must not change what the wire says
without saying so. This is [reactive honesty](/surface/reactive-honesty) one
layer down: a recovered connection must not quietly be talking to something
else.

A peer whose protocol cannot be spoken is taken over, whatever the policy says.
Refusing instead looks principled, because the drain verb really is unreachable.
It is the wrong act: a cross-epoch upgrade then stays unconverged until a person
stops the old daemon by hand.

## Why a runtime fault kills the process

Every shutdown reason the daemon reports is a success except one. When the
served runtime dies, the process tears down in order and then exits non-zero,
because the exit code is the supervisor's only channel for "that was a crash,
not a stop".

Logging the fault and serving on is the tempting alternative, and it produces a
zombie: the process alive, the single-instance gate held, the socket answering,
the runtime behind it dead. A failed poll read is already cell-local at every
tick, so a dead runtime can only be structural wiring death, which does not heal
by waiting.

Kolu met the zombie once. A padi logged a probe timeout, kept its gate and
socket, and needed an operator, where a crash would have been respawned and
restored in about two seconds
([kolu#2101](https://github.com/juspay/kolu/pull/2101)).

## Why the supervisor is a separate package

The daemon code and the supervising code are split into two packages, and the
split is load-bearing. A supervisor runs in the client and must never be able to
change what a daemon restart loads, so it is kept out of the daemon's hashed
closure. The package boundary is the hash boundary. A supervisor edit cannot
look like a new daemon build, because the supervisor is not in the build id.

A supervisor is also written in Effect with no Promise-shaped twin. It composes
its boot, its recycle and its drain into one fiber tree. A boot that loses a
race, a restart you abandoned and a drain past its ceiling each tear their own
work down instead of running on unobserved.

## Why the framework takes values, not loose fields

Three small shapes in this stratum follow one rule: make the wrong combination
impossible to write rather than forbidden in prose.

- A daemon's **home** is a primitive, not a convenience. The daemon and the
  supervisor both take a home and derive the gate and the socket from it, so
  loose path strings never cross from your code into the framework. A command
  line override is absorbed into the home, never sprinkled at call sites.
- Launch mode and environment inheritance are one union field, so "inherit the
  parent environment on a normal launch" is a type error instead of an
  invariant you have to remember.
- A drain hook that refuses is a defect rather than a member error. The frozen
  fragment declares no error schema, because its shape is not a consumer's to
  widen, and an undeclared throw stays a defect instead of posing as something a
  supervisor could narrow on and handle.

## A daemon is not a fleet

Long-lived and runs-on-other-machines look like they travel together. They do
not. [kaval](/kaval) is a daemon on your own machine: one process, no ssh, no
map. It needs everything in this stratum and none of the
[multi-host](/surface/across-the-hosts) one. The spine is what a daemon needs in
order to be recycled honestly. Where the daemon runs is a separate question.

## What freezing costs you

Freezing a control core commits you to a shape for good, because the recycle
story depends on old and new builds agreeing how to ask who they are. Change
that, and you break the one channel meant to survive change.

The stratum takes a small permanent rigidity at the core in exchange for freedom
to replace everything around it. For a process upgraded under load without
dropping its clients, that is the trade worth making, and the narrowness of the
frozen part keeps the price low.
